About

What I'm doing, and why.

I'm a second-year master's student in Applied Physics at National Taiwan University, working in biophysics at Academia Sinica under Prof. Lin Keng-Hui and co-advised by Prof. Lin Li-Chyong. My research is on how epithelial cells sense the curvature of the surface they live on — but the deeper question, the one I keep returning to, is about emergence: how simple local rules give rise to collective structure.

That question follows me around. It's why I find myself reading biophysics, complex systems theory, and Douglas Hofstadter in the same evening. It's why I paint watercolors in the afternoons — a brush touches wet paper and the pigment organizes itself in ways no one planned. It's why I spent a summer teaching myself computer vision to track a tennis ball through the air. The specifics change; the question doesn't.

This site is a place to think in public. I'm using it as a slow, honest record of what I'm reading, making, and working through — research notes, essays, projects that never quite finish. The goal isn't a portfolio. It's a practice: showing up and writing down what I actually think, not what I believe I should think.

If any of that resonates, you're welcome here. Most of what you'll find below is unfinished on purpose.

Research

How do cells decide which way is "down" on a curved surface?

Most of cell biology happens on flat glass. A petri dish is a reasonable approximation of flat, and a cell grown on flat glass is well-behaved and easy to image. But real tissues are not flat. The inside of your gut is corrugated; the surface of a developing embryo is a landscape of hills and valleys; a blood vessel is a cylinder. Cells live on curved surfaces, and there is good reason to think they notice.

My research, done in Prof. [Advisor]'s lab at Academia Sinica with Prof. Lin Li-Chyong as co-advisor, asks what epithelial cells do when you put them on a surface whose curvature you can change. We use a pneumatically actuated PDMS microdome — a thin elastic membrane that we inflate or deflate to switch between flat, convex (dome-out), and concave (dome-in) geometries, all while the same cells sit on top.

The surprising part

The textbook prediction for curvotaxis — directed migration driven by curvature — is that cells prefer concave regions. The reasoning is geometric: on a concave surface, a cell's neighbors are slightly closer than they would be on a flat one, which makes adhesion energetically favorable. This has been observed for single cells in multiple systems.

We see something else. When we grow a confluent MDCK monolayer on a convex dome, the cells migrate toward the top of the dome — centripetally, inward, against the textbook prediction. The effect is robust and reproducible. It tells us that collective migration on curved substrates isn't just single-cell curvotaxis summed up. Something about being part of a sheet changes what the cells do.

The hard part

The hard part is that curvature never comes alone. When you deform a PDMS membrane into a dome, you also strain it — and cells have a separate, well-known response to strain called tensotaxis. So when we see centripetal migration on a dome, we don't immediately know whether the cells are responding to the curvature, the strain, or some combination of the two. Disentangling these is most of what I'm doing right now: designing paired controls (same curvature, different strain; same strain, different curvature) and building the analysis tools to measure each independently.

Tools I've built along the way

One tool that turned out to matter is a Lambert equal-area projection correction for 2D velocity and strain fields measured on curved 3D substrates. When you image a dome from above, pixels far from the apex represent smaller physical areas than pixels near the apex — the projection distorts the measurement. The Lambert projection is the cartographer's trick for unfolding a sphere onto a plane while preserving area, and it turns out to be exactly what you want for unbiased PIV on a curved sample. It's not glamorous, but getting the coordinates right is most of getting the answer right.

Alongside that, I've built the group's Python pipeline for 3D particle tracking (for membrane characterization), 2D PIV on projected images (for collective migration), and bulge- test fitting (for mechanical calibration of the PDMS). A lot of graduate research is just: read the image correctly, then ask it the right question.

Essay · March 2026

When the cells go the wrong way

On what it feels like to find a result that contradicts what you expected to find — and why I'm learning to trust it before I can explain it.

The first time I saw the velocity field pointing inward on a convex dome, I assumed I had a bug. I spent two weeks looking for it. I re-ran the PIV with different parameters, re-ran the Lambert projection in case I had flipped a sign, swapped test datasets, asked a friend to look at my code. Everything checked out. The cells really were migrating toward the top of the dome, and the textbook said they shouldn't be.

I want to write down what that felt like, because I think it matters. It didn't feel triumphant. It felt slightly embarrassing — as if I had been caught disagreeing with a textbook on a subject I was clearly less qualified on than the textbook. My first instinct was not I've found something, it was I've made a mistake I haven't located yet.

A few days later, reading Sadhu et al. (2024), I came across a line in their discussion that said, in effect: collective migration on curved substrates may not reduce to single-cell curvotaxis, because the mechanics of a sheet are different from the mechanics of its parts. And I felt the lock click.

The thing I want to remember is the gap between the observation and the permission to trust the observation. For two weeks I had real data telling me something interesting, and I refused to look at it as data — I looked at it as bugs-in-disguise. The permission to trust it came from outside, from a paper that said something adjacent enough that I could borrow its confidence. I wonder how many findings get filed under "bug I couldn't find" because nobody ever hands the researcher that borrowed permission.

This is a note to future me, when I'm more senior and someone less senior brings me a weird result: the default response should be tell me more, not check your code. Both can be true at once, but the order matters. Check-your-code first kills things that should have lived.

Python tooling I've built for the lab: PIV on projected images, multi-region CSV analysis, velocity-vector visualization, and a Lambert equal-area projection correction so that measurements on curved PDMS membranes aren't distorted by the projection from 3D to 2D. FractalFocus
